Portland, OR, August 2021. Alpine Climbing Season here in the Pacific Northwest. It's been an awfully hot summer. I learn a new word: Heat Dome. Could have done without that one. Retreating up high into the mountains, with green alpine meadows, gurgling creeks, seracs falling somewhere in the distance - that sounds better. So, despite busy schedules, Lindsey and I clear out our calendars for some alpine adventuring again. I have been dreaming about seeing the Northern Cascades for some time, having read some stunning climbing reports. It looks incredible up there. Browsing through guide books and trip reports online increases my excitement, but does not yield any specific destinations. But Lindsey comes through, suggesting a few alternatives, and we ultimately settle for the lofty goal of attempting the Northwest Face of the Forbidden Peak–an impressive feat of alpine climbing, first climbed by Fred Beckey and Ed Cooper in 1959. Browsing through trip reports (all praising the incredible piece of alpine climbing that is the NW Face of Forbidden), it emerges that the crux of the climb would be how to get to the climb. Our research yields two alternative approaches to the base of the climb. From Boston Basin, the proposed base camp location, the obvious (but unpopular) option seems to be climb up to the West Ridge, rappel down on the Forbidden Glacier, traverse to the climb. The "clockwise" way. The more scenic (and objectively safer) way would be a counterclockwise circumnavigation of the mountain: Leave Boston Basin, head east toward the Shark Fin Col, extending the East Ridge of forbidden. The rap down from the col to Forbidden glacier seems to be the crux. Traverse the Boston Glacier, cross the North Ridge, traverse Forbidden Glacier to the base of the climb. Long. Scenic. Perfect. The plan is made.

### Monday, August 2

Monday comes around. We leave Portland in the late morning. As usual, we toss in more gear than we need and begin the endless slog up I-5 north, enhanced by some pan au lait from one of Seattle's fancy bakeries. Interstate changes to highway, highway changes to dirt road. After resolving the gear questions we had been putting off until the last minute (i.e. the trailhead), we are finally headed up into the alpine. Views in the Northern Cascades are the most pristine I have thus far seen in North America. They remind me of the Alps. Steep. Peaks behind peaks behind peaks. Yet different. Wild. We enjoy our evening hike up to Boston basin, passing through layers of cooler and cooler air. After some scouting in the dusk assisted by headlamps, we find a camp spot on a ledge just below the regular boston basin camp.

### Tuesday, August 3

For Tuesday, we set our sights on exploring the Shark Fin Col. On the way up, we were warned about the conditions of the glacier on the rappel from the Col. The crux of the approach (i.e. the crux of climb) might be even cruxier this year– the Quien Sabe Glacier looks less substantial at the beginning of August than it did in the trip report pictures. We shall see. The chossy climb up the col is manageable. We make our way up and are rewarded with a breathtaking view of Forbidden Peak and its east, west and north Ridges. Boston Glacier spreads in front of us, as far as the eyes can see. Now the descent down a dirty, chossy ramp begins. I am scared. This looks sketchy. The objective hazard is high. Discomfort surrounding the sheer complexity and size of the climb is enhanced by the detail, the moves that lie directly ahead. Is this too big to chew? I voice my concerns. Lindsey's calm attitude and rational approach, with just the right amount of kindness and understanding, it's all I need. Take it step by step. fear is the mind killer.

And so we search. Slowly, Safely, Surely, we make our way down the ramp, searching for that rap station that will lead us... where? Onto the Glacier? Well, we don't know. Is the landing stable? Is it still there? We keep searching, until Lindsey suggests we turn back. We have not found the promised rap station. So we turn back. After we make it down the south side of the Col, we stop on the granite slabs to eat some snacks, soak up the alpine sun, cool our feet in a cold glacier run off. What should we do? Given that we have not found the crucial point to get off the col and onto the glacier, I suggest a change of plans. If we go for it, will our attempt ultimately be halted by not being able to get onto the Boston glacier? And what comes after? We haven't even seen what's past the North Ridge and on the Forbidden Glacier. Lindsey agrees to change plans and attempt climbing the more straightforward West Ridge. Is it disappointment I feel? Or relief? A bit of both, probably. So we head back to camp as the clouds roll in from the SW. We manage to cook dinner and retreat into our sleeping bags just as the thunder starts to rumble. My first thunderstorm in a tent. We listen to the rumble, count the time between Lightning and Thunder. Lindsey has a different way of counting, I realize. Is her "One Mississippi, Two Mississippi, Three Mississippi" as accurate as my counting of seconds, as I learned when I was a kid? Is "One Mississippi" really 1.6 seconds long? I ponder this as I doze off.

### Wednesday, August 4

Alarm rings at 4AM. The rain seems to have stopped. We grab our pre-packed bags and cold instant coffee, then make our way up the south ramp towards the West Ridge. On the Slab, we veer right, as described by a climber in the camp the following evening. The ascent feels smooth and swift up the granite slabs, leaping across creeks, nibbling on Sea Wolf pastries generously laced with Grocery Outlet Bargain Market Cream cheese (I bet they don't actually bargain there, ever). We get to the glacier, put on our crampons, pull the axe out and make our way up towards the thin tongue, reaching into the gully that will ultimately lead us to the West Ridge. The Glacier tongue looks thin, but passable. As we climb up, I find myself thinking of the guided group that we knew made their way up the day before us.I keep wondering: did the guide really lead his clients up this way? Up our way? I am not so sure at times. The glacier is melted out on each side and there is a considerable gap between the steep walls of the gully and the glacier. The transition off the tongue that is less than 10 feet wide proves to be tricky. We manage (somehow– thanks, Linds.) and continue up the chossy gully, thankful that there are no parties above or below us. We keep our crampons on and our axes out, as we continue through patches of snow, chossy patches of broken granite, and large blocks cammed in our way, just waiting to be kicked loose. We arrive at what feels like a dead end. I am scared again, either direction looks difficult, dangerous, not usually climbed. I am sure this is not where the guided party went. We must have missed the fork. Fear creeps in again. But I brought Lindsey. Lindsey, glowing with optimism, eager to solve the problem at hand. She would have made a good engineer, I think. The steep traverse to the ridge ahead looks tough and scary. That is, until Linds slaps on her Miuras and makes quick work of it. Within a few minutes, we are up at the ridge. Fear and skepticism makes way for euphoria. We are at the ridge. Ahead of us are six plus pitches of moderate, fun ridge climbing on premium Northern Cascade granite. The weather is perfect. The views are breathtaking. We climb the six pitches (as some sources suggest), and turn it into one blissful, seemingly endless and incredibly enjoyable simul pitch. Lindsey blazes ahead, I wait for the rope between us to tighten, and I follow. Up and up. After what feels like five minutes (but likely around twenty), we reach a pointy fin with a few slings, signifying the end of the dream pitch and likely the first rap. It's almost 9AM. That's a lot of living for a Wednesday morning. I smile from ear to ear, as does Lindsey (forever captured in one of my better pictures of 2021). We made it. Almost. There is a small traverse to what seems to be the actual summit. Just half a pitch away. We go for it, but I don't feel like it adds to the climb. It does not feel "necessary". We are up here– who cares about the last few feet? Then again, where is the sense in any of the vertical feet gained– today, or ever? We climb to the "true summit" for the same reason we came here in the first place. Because it's there.

After spotting a small column of smoke north of us, we call 911 to notify the authorities of a new wildfire. It certainly adds to the accomplishment. Watching airplanes and helicopters checking out the wildfire after our notification as we rappel, it adds a layer of serving the public, and nature to our hedonistic adventure, and we are here for it. Rapping down ridges sucks. If you rappel, your rope gets stuck, and you have to solo up to get it unstuck, then what's the point in rapping in the first place? It is nothing but tedious and slow....is what I would say if I was not scared.

Four (or five) raps, a comfortable little bit of downclimbing, and we are back to our point of entry where we had stashed our crampons and axes. A few more raps down the chossy gully, and we are back to the glacier tongue. We are almost back down. Time to add some unnecessary excitement by dropping my ice axe down the side of the glacier tongue as I rap. What a stupid mistake. I lose the axe, I lose the carabiner– easily prevented by checking if the carabiner closes. But I did not check. And now both are gone. I start to get scared. Again. (Fear is the mind killer.) Let’s focus on the task at hand. Rap down the semi-scary piece of tat, down to the bottom of the chasm between glacier and mountain. Lindsey is down ahead, looking for a way back onto the glacier. I land, I see the axe. Is it recoverable? I start working on the glacier that separates me from my axe. First with feet, then with a piece of rock. Turns out– glacier ice is quite solid. This will take hours. The objective hazard in this gap is too high. Abandon the axe, accepting a risky descent down the glacier? Or find another way? I spot an opening about 30ft higher up. Up the rappel, I make my way into the glacier. Freshly melted glacier water soaks my sun shirt. It's cold. I only wear a thin layer. But the adrenaline from losing my axe and crawling into a tiny hole under a glacier prevents me from really feeling the cold. I get down on my knee, down on my belly, I fumble for the axe. I got it. I got it back. It's in my hand. I will not let it go this time.

I make my way out, feeling dizzy, and reach for my water in the pack. "NOW IS NOT THE TIME FOR DRINKING". Lindseys voice from further down snaps me back into the moment. We need to get out of here. And Lindsey found a way. We make our way back up on the glacier. One foot on rock, one foot buried in the side of the glacier. One hand holding onto rock, one hacking into the solid snow. How would I have done this without an axe? Does not matter. I don't have to. I retrieved my axe. And I won't lose it again.

Some mellow descent, a rappel, and a long glissade that makes my glutes feel numb later: we sit again on the granite slabs, smoothed over by an eternity of glacier coverage, soaking our feet, enjoying the afternoon sun. We made it up. We made it back down. 12 hours. A number that does not seem to accurately reflect the amount of life that has been lived within that time span. Time is relative, I suppose. I feel grateful. Grateful I was allowed to be there, up on the mountain, today. Grateful for Lindsey, my climbing partner, guide, lifetime friend. Grateful for the sun on my face, the warm granite under my butt. Grateful to be on this journey.
